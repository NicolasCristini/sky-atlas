### crux-ancient: Reword
Wikipedia "Crux" (opened): the Greeks knew the stars and Ptolemy counted them as part of Centaurus; they "were visible as far north as Britain in the fourth millennium BC"; precession lowered them, and by 400 AD they never rose over most of Europe. Cadamosto, after leaving the Gambia River in 1455, noted what was probably the Southern Cross, calling it "carro dell'ostro" (his diagram was inaccurate). João Faras (letter of 1 May 1500) is credited with the first correct European depiction; Vespucci (1501–02) seems to have observed it.
My calculation (cross-check only): Acrux's declination was about −35° around 3000 BC, so visible to about 54°N, and about −52° at 1 AD, visible to about 38°N. This is consistent with the source.
Corrected wording: "Thousands of years ago the Southern Cross rose in the northern sky, and Ptolemy listed its stars as part of Centaurus. Precession slowly sank them below the horizon of Europe (by about 400 AD across most of it), and Europeans met them again in the south, as the Venetian Cadamosto did off West Africa in 1455."
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crux (opened — confirms, secondary); Alvise Cadamosto, *Navigazioni* (not opened).

### nocturnal: Confirmed
Royal Museums Greenwich (opened): nocturnals were used by mathematicians, astronomers and navigators "from about 1400 to 1800"; the first printed description is Peter Apian's *Cosmographia* (1524); most were made 1500–1700; the Ursa Major pointer stars act as an hour hand, with extra scales for the Little Bear's pointer stars for navigators at lower latitudes.
Source: https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/astronomy/what-nocturnal (opened — confirms).

### decans: Reword
McMaster "Ancient Egyptian Astronomy" database (opened): the coffin is from Asyut; the table has 36 decan columns (plus triangle decans); the page gives no dynasty and cites Pogo (1932) for "second half of the third millennium". Scientific American (opened): 27 known tables, most "about 2100 B.C., from the Middle Kingdom", found at Asyut; each column is a ten-day week; 12 rows are the "hours" of the night, short and irregular; Neugebauer and Parker proposed the "star clock" reading in the 1960s, and Symons now sees them as more like almanacs. Neither page addresses the 24-hour day.
Corrected wording: "From about 2100 BC, Egyptian coffin lids were painted with tables of 36 star groups called decans, each one marking ten days, with 12 rows for the 'hours' of the night. Egyptian night and day hours changed length through the year, and this 12-and-12 split is an ancestor of our 24 hours."
Note: older books place Asyut lids in the First Intermediate Period (9th–10th Dynasty); sources disagree on the label.
Sources: https://aea.mcmaster.ca/index.php/en/database/diagonal-star-tables/t1-s1c-eat-1-coffin-1 (opened — partial); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/decoding-the-star-charts-of-ancient-egypt/ (opened — confirms); Neugebauer and Parker, *Egyptian Astronomical Texts* I (not opened).

### four-minutes: Confirmed
Wikipedia "Sidereal time" (opened): sidereal day 23 h 56 min 4.09 s; the solar day is about 3 min 56 s longer, because Earth moves about 1° around the Sun during one rotation. Two hours per month is arithmetic (about 30 x 3.93 min).
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_time (opened — confirms, secondary).

### hesiod: Confirmed
Perseus (Evelyn-White, opened): WD 383–387, Pleiades rising signals harvest (footnote: early May), setting signals ploughing (about November); they are hidden for forty nights and days. WD 564–565: sixty days after the solstice Arcturus rises (footnote: February–March). WD 609–617: Orion and Sirius reach midheaven, Dawn sees Arcturus, pick the grapes; Pleiades, Hyades and Orion setting mean time to plough. Wikipedia "Pleiades" (opened) places Hesiod's Pleiades lines around 618–623. Note WD 609–610 is a harvest-timing cue.
Sources: Perseus Hesiod pages http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0132:card=383, card=564 and card=609 (opened — confirm).

### zodiac-babylon: Confirmed
Wikipedia "Zodiac" (opened): around the end of the 5th century BC Babylonian astronomers divided the ecliptic into 12 equal signs of 30°, introduced between 409 and 398 BC (probably about 401 BC); the ecliptic crosses 13 constellations in the IAU boundaries (including Ophiuchus); precession has moved the March equinox from Aries into Pisces since Babylonian times.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac (opened — confirms, secondary); Hunger and Pingree, *Astral Sciences in Mesopotamia* (not opened).

### sirius-nile: Reword
Wikipedia "Sothic cycle" (opened): Sirius's return "was considered to herald the annual flooding of the Nile"; the civil year of 365 days had no leap year until 22 BCE and slipped about one day every four years; a Sothic cycle is 1,461 civil years (1,460 Julian years); Censorinus recorded a rising on Egyptian New Year's Day between 139 and 142 CE (calculation gives 20 July 139 CE, Julian); observations were made at Heliopolis/Memphis, Thebes and Elephantine, and the rising at Cairo came about 8 days after Aswan. The page gives no invisibility period. A search summary of Teije de Jong's Brill chapter says Sirius reappears after about 70 days of invisibility at the latitude of Memphis (not opened); a Simple Wikipedia user page says it was never 70 days at Elephantine or Thebes. Modern Cairo date: skipped as asked.
Corrected wording: "In ancient Egypt, Sirius (Sopdet; Greek Sothis) disappeared from the sky for roughly two months, then reappeared just before dawn in mid-July (Julian), about when the Nile began to rise, and this marked the new year. The 365-day civil calendar slipped by a day every four years, so it only matched Sirius again around 139 CE."
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sothic_cycle (opened — confirms, secondary); Teije de Jong, "The Heliacal Rising of Sirius" https://researchgate.net/profile/Teije-De-Jong-2/publication/275033106_The_Heliacal_Rising_of_Sirius/links/55310a600cf27acb0de91c22/The-Heliacal-Rising-of-Sirius.pdf (403, search only); Censorinus, *De Die Natali* 18 (not opened).

### dog-days: Confirmed
Wikipedia "Dog days" (opened): Latin *dies caniculares* from Greek; the Greeks linked Sirius's reappearance with the hottest part of summer and poets believed the star brought heat and fever; Hesiod treated the days before Sirius reappeared as the worst; reckoned from about 19 July (Julian) in antiquity; modern sources give 3 July to 11 August.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dog_days (opened — confirms, secondary).

### latitude-sailing: Reword

Corrected wording: "In the 15th century Portuguese pilots found their latitude from the height of the Pole Star, using a quadrant or astrolabe; after crossing the equator they used the Sun's noon height and tables. Sources: https://ecuip.lib.uchicago.edu/diglib/science/cultural_astronomy/cultures_portugal-2.html (opened — confirms); Oxford Cabinet page (could not open).

### canopus-posidonius: Reword
Wikipedia "Canopus" (opened): declination about −52°41'; northern limit about 37°18'N; not visible to the Greeks and Romans on the mainland; Eratosthenes and Ptolemy recorded it from Alexandria. ### eratosthenes: Reword
Wikipedia "Eratosthenes" (opened): 7.2° (1/50 of a circle); about 5,000 stadia from bematists; 5,000 x 50 gives about 250,000 stadia (Cleomedes); Roman sources of the 1st century CE give 252,000; stadion lengths from 160 m to 210 m, with 185 m (Attic) among them; with a 180 m stadion about 40,338 km, within 1%; the method was in his lost *On the Measure of the Earth*; he assumed Syene was on the Tropic and on Alexandria's meridian, whereas it is about 1° north of the Tropic and 3° east of Alexandria; the "well" quote is cited to Khan Academy, a modern source. I earlier said the well is from Strabo; I did not verify that.
Corrected wording: "About 240 BC Eratosthenes compared the noon Sun at Syene (Aswan), where it was said to be overhead at midsummer, with Alexandria, where it was about 7.2° (1/50 of a circle) from overhead. With a distance of about 5,000 stadia he got roughly 250,000 stadia for the Earth's circumference; how close that is to the true 40,000 km depends on which stadion he used, from under 1% off to about 16% off."

### zenith-stars: Confirmed
Polynesian Voyaging Society page (opened): "The zenith star of Hawai'i is Hokule'a (Arcturus); the zenith star of Tahiti is 'A'a (Sirius)"; "The declination of the zenith star is equal to the latitude it is associated with"; Hōkūleʻa about 19°N (South Point, Hawaiʻi Island) and ʻAʻā about 17°S (Tahiti). Spelling: write Hōkūleʻa, ʻAʻā, Hawaiʻi with the ʻokina; the PVS page uses plain apostrophes.
Source: https://archive.hokulea.com/navigate/latitude.html (opened — confirms).

### hokulea-1976: Confirmed
PVS archive account (Ben Finney) (opened): Hōkūleʻa left Honolua Bay, Maui, on 1 May 1976 and reached Pape'ete, Tahiti, on 4 June 1976 (34 days; Mataiva sighted 1 June); navigator Mau Piailug, from Satawal, a Carolinian atoll in Micronesia; navigated "without any instruments or other aids"; part of the American Bicentennial. PVS "Our story" page (opened): more than 17,000 people were on the beach at Papeʻete; Mau trained Nainoa Thompson, who repeated the voyage in 1980. The Hōkūleʻa canoe page says it was launched 8 March 1975.
Sources: https://archive.hokulea.com/holokai/1976/ben_finney.html (opened — confirms); https://worldwidevoyage.hokulea.com/voyages/our-story/ (opened — confirms); https://hokulea.com/voyaging-canoes/ (opened — launch date only).
